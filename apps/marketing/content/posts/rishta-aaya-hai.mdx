Dear family,

In most of our homes, the phrase arrives before the person does. _Rishta aaya hai_: a proposal has arrived. Someone's aunt knows someone's cousin, a biodata is forwarded, and for a few evenings the whole house reads one page very carefully.

We grew up with that page. It lists the things families actually talk about: where the family is from, what the parents do, how many siblings, the languages spoken at home, whether the household is vegetarian, what the person hopes for. It is read top to bottom, slowly, and usually more than once.

We also grew up with what happened next. The page went into a WhatsApp group of forty people. Photographs travelled further than anyone intended. Nobody could say no without it feeling like an insult, so people said nothing at all. And when our friends tried the apps instead, they were asked to decide about a person in two seconds, with a swipe, and to call it a match.

## What we kept, and what we took away

Rishta keeps the page and the family, and removes the parts everyone privately dislikes.

**You read a small folio each evening.** Five to seven complete pages, chosen against the things you won't compromise on. You read them one at a time. Turning a page never answers it, and nothing is decided by a swipe.

**Interest costs a sentence.** If a page moves you, you write a note of at least forty characters and press your seal onto it. It is a small effort on purpose: the person reading your note deserves to know you read their page.

**Both seals break together.** Photos, full names, workplaces and contact stay sealed until both of you say yes. Then both seals break at once, the sealed sections open to each other, and we propose three evenings for a first call, set in both of your time zones.

**Family writes in pencil.** Your mother can read a page through a private link, in Hindi or Urdu or Punjabi or whichever language she reads, without making an account. Under the page she has three buttons: Proceed, Let's talk, and Not for us. Her answer appears beside the page for you to see. She advises; you decide. Only you can write a note or say yes.

**No is said kindly.** Declining sends a short, gracious note instead of silence. When a conversation ends, it closes with a line of thanks. Nobody is left wondering.

## What you will not find

There are no compatibility percentages, because a percentage is a guess dressed up as a fact. Instead, each page in your folio comes with a few plain reasons it is there, and the honest gaps: _"Diet differs"_, _"Community: not stated on his page."_

There are no bids, no boosts and no counts of how many people are interested in anyone. People are not inventory. Priority notes exist, but they are flat, they cost one credit, and they are always labelled for the reader.

There are no background checks, and we say so plainly: Rishta does not conduct criminal background checks on its members. We do verify email addresses, we veil photos on our servers so strangers never receive the clear picture, and every report is read by a person.

## Who it is for

We built Rishta first for second-generation South Asian families in the US, the UK and Canada, because the biodata is already part of how those families meet. It is also for people marrying again, after a divorce or a loss, who are poorly served by apps that assume a first marriage. Nothing about it is exclusive: the same page has cousins in the shidduch résumé and the Muslim marriage CV, and we hope to welcome those families too.

## Designed to end

A good matchmaker hopes never to see you again. So do we. When you are engaged, you tell us, and we close your page kindly and let your open conversations know. That is the whole point.

If you would like to begin, a page takes an evening to write, and nothing about you is visible to anyone until you publish it. If a relative starts it for you, it stays invisible until you confirm it.

With respect,
